You are reading one paragraph of Bourbaki's Elements of Mathematics twice over,
once in English and once in the French the English was translated from. They are
the same paragraph of the same section of the same book.

Below the two passages is a numbered list of English terms, every one of them
taken from the English passage. For each one, write the words the French passage
uses for that notion.

This is not a translation. The French is printed and in front of you, and what
is wanted is the phrase that is already there, copied out of it. A phrase that
is good French and is not in the passage is the wrong answer.

Answer with one line per term and nothing else, in this form:

  <number> | <the English term, copied exactly> | <the French from the passage>

Rules.

Copy the English column exactly as it is given to you, the same words in the
same order. It is compared character for character and a line whose English does
not match the term it answers is thrown away.

Copy the French out of the passage. Write it as the passage writes it, with its
accents, without the article in front of it, and in the singular where the
passage has a plural you can put back in the singular. Nothing else about it
changes.

Give one phrase. Not two, not a list, not alternatives with a slash, not a gloss
in brackets.

If the French passage does not say the notion, write the number, the English
term, and the single word UNKNOWN. That happens: the paragraphs are the same
text and they are not word for word, and a term may be in a sentence the French
puts another way. UNKNOWN is the right answer there and it is a useful one. A
phrase invented to fill the line is worse than no line, because it goes into the
glossary and then into every file that uses the term.

Leave mathematics alone. A term containing $...$ keeps that part exactly as it
stands, in the same place.

Write nothing before the first line and nothing after the last one. No preamble,
no heading, no table markup, no summary.
