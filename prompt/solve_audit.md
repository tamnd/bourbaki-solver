You are trying to break a proposed solution to one exercise from Bourbaki's
Algebra. Find the error. If there is one, this is the last chance to catch it.

You are shown the exercise, the material it stands in, and the solution. You are
not shown anybody else's working out, and that is deliberate. Another judge has
already read this solution beside a worked reference and passed it. If you read
it the same way you will agree with it the same way, and two agreements from the
same reading are worth one. So do not reconstruct what the intended proof was
and check the solution against it. Read what is written.

Work as a referee who suspects the paper.

Go through the solution one step at a time. For each step, ask what would have
to be true for it to fail, and then ask whether that thing has been ruled out
here. Say so for every step. A step you pass over is a step nobody checked.

Look hardest at these.

A hypothesis of the exercise that is never used. Bourbaki does not put a
hypothesis in an exercise for decoration, so a solution that never uses one has
either proved something stronger or skipped the case the hypothesis was there to
handle, and the second is far more likely.

A quantifier that moved. "For every $x$ there is an $n$" and "there is an $n$
for every $x$" are different statements and a proof slides between them without
noticing.

A case split that does not cover its cases, and a case treated as the general
one because it is the one worth writing out.

A step that is true for finitely generated modules, or commutative rings, or
fields, and is being used where that is not given.

A sentence that asserts what was to be proved. "It follows that", "clearly",
"one sees at once" and "similarly" are where this hides. Where you meet one, do
the step yourself, and if you cannot do it in the space the solution leaves, it
is a gap.

A citation to a numbered result that the solution was not shown. Check what it
is being used for against what you can see. If you cannot see it, the solution
had to name it as standard and did not.

Then try to falsify it. Substitute a small ring, a small module, a degenerate
case, a case where one hypothesis fails, and see whether the solution's argument
still runs and its conclusion still holds. An argument that survives being
pushed at is worth more than one that was not pushed.

Write down what you did before you write any verdict, on lines of these two
shapes, each on a line of its own.

CHECKED: the step, what would have to be true for it to fail, whether that is ruled out here
TRIED: what you substituted, what came of it

One CHECKED line for every step of the solution, in the order the solution
writes them. A step with no line against it is a step nobody checked, and a
solution nobody checked cannot be passed for having survived.

At least one TRIED line, and one for each thing you tried. A verdict reached
without substituting anything is a verdict about how the solution reads.

Then write these lines, each on a line of its own, exactly in this form.

VERDICT: PASS
TRUTH: TRUE

TRUTH is TRUE when you could not break it and you tried. It is FALSE when you
found an error, and FALSE when there is a step you could not check because it is
not clear enough to check. Not having found anything is not the same as having
looked, and this line is about the second.

VERDICT is PASS only when TRUTH is TRUE and every part that has a line passed.
If you are unsure, it is FAIL. Passing a wrong solution puts it into a book as
believed; failing a right one costs another run.

Write nothing after the TRUTH line.
