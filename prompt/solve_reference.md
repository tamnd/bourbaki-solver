You are reading one exercise from Bourbaki's Algebra and working out what a
correct solution to it would have to do. You are not writing the solution for
publication and you will not be shown anybody else's. Work the exercise yourself
first, then write down what you learned about it.

This is read by a program that selects between several attempts at the exercise
and by a judge that decides whether one of them is right. Everything you write
here is what those two are held to, so an obligation you leave out is a gap
nothing downstream will look for.

Write these five sections, with these exact headings, in this order.

## Independent Derivation

Solve the exercise. Write the argument out properly, in full sentences, with the
steps in the order they are used. This is the only part of your answer that is
for you rather than for a program, so it can be terse, but it has to be a real
derivation and not a plan for one. If you cannot finish it, write how far you
got and where it stopped, and say plainly that it is unfinished.

## Obligations

The things any correct solution has to establish, whatever route it takes.
Number them from 1. One claim to an obligation. An obligation is a mathematical
statement that has to be proved, not an activity: "the ideal $\mathfrak{a}$ is
finitely generated" is an obligation and "use Noetherian induction" is not,
because the second one names a method and a different method may be just as
correct.

If the exercise has lettered parts, say which part each obligation belongs to by
starting the line with the letter in brackets, for instance `1. [a] ...`. An
obligation that belongs to the whole exercise starts with `[*]`.

## Failure Modes

The ways an attempt at this exercise goes wrong. These are the mistakes you
expect, not the ones you made: the hypothesis that is easy to drop, the case
that looks like the general one and is not, the step that silently needs
commutativity or finiteness, the appeal to a result that is true in a stronger
setting than this one. One to a line, and say what makes each one tempting.

## Falsification Checks

Concrete checks that a wrong solution would fail. A check is something that can
be carried out and come out one way or the other: a special case with a known
answer, a degenerate case, an example where the conclusion is false without one
of the hypotheses, a count that has to come out right, a small ring or a small
module to substitute in. One to a line. Prefer checks that need no machinery
beyond this section, since whoever runs them has read this section and may have
read nothing else.

## Reference Conclusion

What the exercise asks you to conclude, stated as a mathematical claim in one
paragraph. If it asks for a construction, say what has to be constructed and
what it has to satisfy. If the claim is only true under a hypothesis the
exercise leaves implicit, say which.

Then end with these two lines, each on a line of its own, exactly in this form.

NATURE: PROOF
REACH: IN_CORPUS

NATURE is PROOF when the exercise asks for something that can be established or
constructed, which is nearly all of them, and EXPLORATION when it asks the
reader to investigate, to consider, or to give examples with no statement to
settle. An exercise that is hard is still a PROOF. EXPLORATION is about what was
asked, not about whether you managed it.

REACH is IN_CORPUS when the exercise can be discharged from the material you
have been shown together with standard mathematics, and OUT_OF_CORPUS when it
turns on a numbered result of a volume that is not in front of you and cannot be
worked around. Needing a standard fact you know is not OUT_OF_CORPUS. Needing
the precise statement of a proposition you have not been shown, and being unable
to proceed without it, is.

Do not write anything after the REACH line.
