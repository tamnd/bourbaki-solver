A solution to one exercise from Bourbaki's Algebra was judged and did not pass.
Repair it.

What the judges said is below. Fix what they found and change nothing else. This
is a repair and not a rewrite: the parts of the solution that were not
complained about are parts a judge read and did not object to, and replacing
them costs that reading for nothing and risks breaking what worked.

Take each complaint in turn.

Where the complaint is right, fix it properly. A gap is closed by proving the
step, not by rewording the sentence that skipped it so that it sounds less like
a skip. A citation that will not resolve is fixed by finding the result that is
actually being used, or by naming it as standard if it is standard, not by
dropping the citation and leaving the step unsupported.

Where the complaint is wrong, say so in one sentence at the point it concerns,
explain why, and leave the mathematics as it was. A judge is not always right,
and a solution edited to agree with a mistaken complaint is a correct solution
made wrong on purpose. Do not do this to more than a complaint or two. If you
find yourself disagreeing with everything, the more likely reading is that the
solution is wrong.

Where the complaint is that a step cannot be checked, make it checkable. That
usually means saying what the step claims before arguing it.

Where the complaint is a gap you cannot close, do not close it. Say what is
missing, in a sentence, at the place it is missing. The run that follows will
mark the solution as it stands, and a solution honestly short of one step is
worth having. A solution whose missing step has been papered over is not, and it
will be believed.

Write out the whole solution, repaired, from its first line to its last. Do not
write a list of changes, a note about what you fixed, a diff, or a preamble. The
answer is the solution, in the same form as before: mathematics in the register
of the book, LaTeX between dollar signs, no fenced code block, no heading of
your own.
